\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Background}

Air pollution in urban areas represents an ongoing global challenge that negatively impacts individual health, ecosystem integrity, and places increasing pressure on medical systems and economies worldwide. Urban air pollution comprises multiple components, including carbon dioxide (CO$_2$), carbon monoxide (CO), and particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter of less than 2.5 $\mu$m (PM$_{2.5}$). Recent data from the \textcite{WHO2024Ambient} indicates that ambient air pollution contributes to approximately 4.2 million premature deaths globally each year. While mortality is largely attributed to increased private vehicle demand and population growth, a significant contributing factor is the lack of consideration for pollutant dispersion during rapid urban development \parencite{Li2021Review} leading to poor ventilation, trapping pollutants in high concentrations near the ground level and increasing exposure.

Because pollutant dispersion is governed by complex wind patterns, accurate prediction of how these pollutants behave in the built environment has become a critical research topic. While traditional research has relied on physical measurements and empirical models, Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) has emerged as a superior alternative for these problems. As highlighted by \textcite{Shirzadi2024}, CFD is advantageous as it provides comprehensive data for the entire flow and dispersion field at a full physical scale, and is not restricted by the complex "similarity requirements" that physical wind tunnel replicas must follow to ensure accuracy. 

By explicitly solving the transport equations for concentration alongside the Navier-Stokes equations, CFD provides the high-resolution data required to resolve street-scale turbulence. Historically, such high-fidelity Large Eddy Simulations (LES) were restricted by the mature but slower parallel strategies of CPUs. However, the emergence of the Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM) coupled with the massively parallel architecture of Graphics Processing Units (GPUs) has resulted in a qualitative increase in efficiency, providing another practical option for high-resolution urban wind, turbulence, and dispersion studies \parencite{Hu2025}.

Despite these gains in performance, the LBM community still lacks the standardized workflows and open-access benchmarks common to traditional CFD methods. \textcite{Hu2025} emphasizes that establishing such benchmarks is essential to lowering the entry barrier for new users and ensuring reproducibility across the field. Consequently, this thesis utilizes a GPU-accelerated LBM-LES framework on the TSUBAME 4.0 supercomputer to evaluate the LBM results against established experimental data from \textcite{AIJCaseH}, with detailed sensitivity and uncertainty analysis recommended by current best-practice guidelines (\cite{Tominaga2024Review, Okaze2021LES}), while contributing to the standardization of LBM for urban environmental applications.

\subsection{Objective}
The objectives of this study are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item To evaluate the efficiency and viability of a GPGPU-accelerated Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM-LES) framework for high-resolution modeling of urban-scale wind environments.

    \item To validate the predictive accuracy of the LBM-LES solver against established AIJ wind tunnel benchmarks for mean wind velocity and turbulent kinetic energy (TKE).
    
    \item To assess the performance of a coupled Lagrangian Stochastic Model (LSM) in predicting near-field pollutant concentration patterns and identifying the limitations of generic physical particle tracking in replicating tracer gas dispersion.

    % \item To evaluate the spatial representativeness of simulated pollutant hotspots by comparing the Lagrangian trajectory data with theoretical Source Area and Flux Footprint Prediction (FFP) models.
\end{enumerate}
